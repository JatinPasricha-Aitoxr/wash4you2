The dry soil comes out before any water goes in

A rug that has spent two years on a Gurugram floor is holding more dry grit than most people expect — NCR road dust, construction fines from the tower next door, and whatever the balcony door lets in through the winter. That grit sits at the base of the pile, below the level you can see or feel, and it is abrasive. Every footstep grinds it against the wool and shortens the life of the rug.

It also has to leave before anything wet arrives, because water turns dry soil into mud and drives it into the foundation. So the first stage of a proper rug wash is purely mechanical: the rug is dusted from the reverse, repeatedly, until nothing more falls out. This is the single biggest difference between a rug that is washed and a rug that has been shampooed where it lay. The shampoo-only job looks good for a fortnight and then goes flat.

Fibre identification decides the method, not the price

Wool, silk, cotton, jute and polypropylene all behave differently in water, so we identify the fibre before we commit to a method. The one to be careful about is viscose — sold as art silk, banana silk or faux silk, and very common in rugs bought off a showroom floor. Viscose is regenerated cellulose. It loses a large part of its strength when wet, the pile can dry stiff and yellowed, and once it browns the colour is inside the fibre rather than on it. If your rug is viscose we will tell you the realistic outcome before we take it, not after.

Dye stability

Hand-knotted Persian, Kashmiri and Indian pieces often carry reds and indigos that were never fully fixed. Every colour is tested with a damp white cloth before washing, and unstable dyes are held with an acidic rinse. Bleeding that has already happened in an earlier wash — the pink halo around a red medallion — can sometimes be reduced. It cannot always be removed.

What cannot be brought back

Bleach spots and sun fade are dye loss, not soil. No cleaning process puts colour back into a fibre; that is a re-dyeing job for a weaver. Pile crush in the traffic lane of a machine-made polypropylene rug is heat-set fibre — the colour will lift, the pile height will not. Moth damage: washing kills larvae and removes the keratin they feed on, but bare patches stay bare.

And on wool, be realistic about spills that are themselves dyes. Wool is a protein fibre and accepts acid dyes readily, which is exactly what children's fruit drinks, food colouring, Holi colour and some red wines are. They bond the way dye is meant to. We usually improve them substantially. We do not promise to erase them.

Pet urine is chemistry, not a stain

The odour lives in the foundation and backing, not in the pile, which is why topical sprays only postpone it. It has to be flushed and rinsed right through. Urine is also alkaline enough to strip dye out of wool permanently — that yellow or orange patch left behind is dye damage, and cleaning will not reverse it.

Drying is where rugs are actually ruined

Wool held damp in Gurugram monsoon humidity develops odour and cellulosic browning within a day. Cotton foundations shrink unevenly when one end dries faster than the other, and the rug comes back wavy and out of square. Controlled, even, fast drying matters more than the wash itself.

How it works

From your door and back again

Inspection and fibre test

Before anything else we identify the fibre from the underside or a stray fringe thread — wool, silk, viscose, cotton, jute or synthetic. This decides the entire method. We also note existing damage with you: worn fringe, curling edges, old pet spots, sun fade, prior repairs.

Colour bleed test

Every colour in the rug is tested with a damp white cloth. Reds and indigos in older hand-knotted pieces are the usual risk. Anything unstable is treated so the dye holds through the wash instead of migrating into the ivory ground.

Dry soil removal

The rug is dusted from the reverse, repeatedly, until nothing more falls out. Dry grit at the base of the pile must leave before water arrives, otherwise it becomes mud in the foundation. This stage takes longer than people expect and matters more than any other.

Spot pre-treatment

Individual spots are worked on their own chemistry — protein, tannin, oil and dye-type spills each need a different approach. We do this before the general wash so we are not fighting a wet rug and a stubborn spot at the same time.

Washing

Method follows fibre. Durable wool and cotton pieces take a full wash and flush; silk, viscose and unstable-dye rugs get a controlled low-moisture treatment instead. Machine-made synthetics take hot water extraction. Fitted wall-to-wall carpet is cleaned in your home.

Rinse and pH balance

Detergent left behind attracts soil and makes the rug re-dirty faster than before, so the rinse is a real stage, not an afterthought. Wool is finished slightly acidic, which is the condition the fibre and its dyes are happiest in.

Controlled drying

The rug is dried fast and evenly, with air moving through it. This is where rugs get ruined in Gurugram — wool held damp in monsoon humidity picks up odour and browning within a day, and a cotton foundation that dries unevenly will shrink out of square.

Grooming, finishing and return

The pile is groomed back into its natural direction, fringes are finished, and the rug gets a final inspection against the notes taken at pickup. It is wrapped, packed on its own away from every other customer's order, and delivered back to your door.
