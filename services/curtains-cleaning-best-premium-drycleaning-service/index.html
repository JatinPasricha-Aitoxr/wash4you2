    Dry soil first, water later

    The most important step happens before anything gets wet. A curtain that has hung for a year in Gurugram holds a surprising amount of dry soil: construction dust from the tower next door, road grit, and in kitchen-facing rooms a fine oily film that the dust sticks to. Put that straight into water and the dust turns to mud and drives deeper into the weave instead of lifting out. So every panel is dry-soil cleared first: heaviest at the header, and along the bottom 20 cm, always the dirtiest part of a floor-length curtain.

    If your curtains look faintly striped once they are down, that is not fading. Dust only lands on the outward face of each pleat, so a pleated panel soils in vertical bands. It comes out.

    What actually decides the method

    The care label matters less than what is inside the panel. We check three things at your window: whether the lining is detachable or sewn in, whether there is an interlining (the soft bump layer inside heavy drapes), and what kind of blackout you have. Coated three-pass blackout and a separate blackout lining behave nothing alike in water. Interlined drapes we do not wet clean at all, because the inner layer shifts and bunches inside the panel and there is no getting it flat again.

    Cotton and linen shrink. Lined cotton shrinks unevenly, because face fabric and lining move at different rates, and you end up with a rippled hem and a drop that is suddenly short of the floor. That is why a lot of curtain work is dry cleaned rather than washed, even when the label says washable.

    What cannot be saved

    Better you know before we take them down.

    • Sun rot. UV degrades the fibre itself, worst on the leading vertical edge and the top few inches. The cloth looks fine, then tears like wet paper. Nothing reverses it, and cleaning is usually what exposes it, so we pinch-test suspect panels with you standing there.
    • Perished blackout coating. Once the rubberised backing has gone tacky or chalky with age and heat, it sheds during cleaning. It cannot be re-coated.
    • Old mildew spotting on linings, from monsoon damp or condensation near a split AC. The mould has stained the fibre, and bleaching it out would take the cloth with it.
    • Heat-set yellowing on polyester sheers. Optical whiteners work on cotton. They do very little for oxidised polyester.

    Before we arrive

    Clear the sill and the pelmet, and tell us if any rod or bracket feels loose. Plastic wall plugs in drywall let go easily, and our terms exclude fixtures that were already weak. We would far rather flag it than discover it.

    One thing people regret skipping: poore room ke parde ek saath, clean every panel in a room and not only the marked one. A freshly cleaned panel hanging beside one that was left will not match, and you cannot unsee it.

    Hooks, rings and hem weights come off, counted and logged with you at the door like any other order, on a written receipt. On rehang we dress the pleats by hand and tie them loosely so the folds set.

    What we take

    Items we handle

    • Sheers, voiles and netsLight polyester voile cleans well and comes back crisp. Older cotton or silk sheers that have hung in direct sun are the single most fragile thing we handle, and we will test before accepting them.
    • Blackout curtainsHandled differently depending on whether the blackout is a coated three-pass fabric or a separate lining sewn behind the face cloth. Coated blackout that has started to feel tacky or chalky is already past saving.
    • Heavy lined and interlined drapesVelvets, jacquards and thick cotton drapes with a lining or a bump interlining. These are dry cleaned rather than washed, because water moves the layers against each other and they will not sit flat again.
    • Cotton and linen curtainsThe ones most likely to shrink, particularly if they have never been cleaned since stitching. We choose the method around the shrinkage risk, not around the label.
    • Roman blindsDowels and cords are removed and reinserted after cleaning, and the fold spacing is set again on rehang so the blind stacks evenly.
    • Valances, pelmets and tiebacksCleaned alongside the main panels so they finish at the same shade. Padded or buckram-stiffened pelmets are assessed individually, as the stiffener can collapse.
    • Door curtains and partition drapesThe heaviest-handled fabric in most homes, with soiling concentrated at hand height on the leading edge. That edge gets hand attention before the main clean.

    How it works

    From your door and back again

    Survey at the window

    Before anything comes down we look at the lining, the header tape, the weights and the state of the rod, and pinch-test any panel that looks sun-weakened. Anything we think will not survive cleaning gets flagged there and then, with you present.

    Takedown and logging

    Panels come off the rod, hooks and rings are removed and bagged, and every piece is counted with you at the door. You get a written receipt of exactly what was collected. Your order is packed on its own and never shares a load with another household's.

    Dry soil removal

    Dust is lifted out dry before any liquid touches the fabric, working the header and the bottom hem hardest. Wetting a dusty curtain simply turns the soil to mud and pushes it into the weave.

    Fabric and colour testing

    Lining and face fabric are tested separately, because they are often different fibres with different tolerances. Printed cottons are checked for dye bleed on a hidden seam allowance before the main clean.

    Cleaning to suit the construction

    Washable panels are cleaned gently in water; dry-clean-only fabrics, interlined drapes and coated blackout go the solvent route. Spot work on marks such as water tidemarks and hem soiling is done by hand before the main process, not after.

    Drying and finishing

    Panels are dried in a controlled way to hold the drop, then pressed flat with the header tape and the hem given particular attention. Pleats are re-formed rather than left to fall as they will.

    Rehang and dressing

    Hooks go back at the same spacing, panels go back on the same rods, and the pleats are dressed by hand into even folds and tied loosely so they set. We do not leave a bundle of curtains at your door for you to sort out.
